TL;DR: Winning is not about reading/doing everything. It's about what you read or do.
𝐃𝐢𝐬𝐜𝐥𝐚𝐢𝐦𝐞𝐫
Be prepared for a tangent-ridden discursive whose nature perhaps couldn't be more antithetical to the spirit of Seneca's essay, "On Discursiveness in Reading". This irony is not lost on me. My original goal was to provide succinct advice for google summer of code students on writing applications. I ended up detailing my complex relationship with one of my favorite, provocative essays by Seneca.

𝐖𝐡𝐲 𝐒𝐞𝐧𝐞𝐜𝐚?
As caretaker of one of the world's largest libraries, it's only right that my advice to GSoC candidates should come directly from section #3 of Seneca the Younger's Epistulae Morales ad Lucilium: "On Discursiveness in Reading" https://en.wikisource.org/.../Moral_letters_to.../Letter_2. I find Seneca has profound messages to share which are easy to miss and easy to disagree with if we take him too literally and afford him no liberties. But if we have the patience to ask, "Why is Seneca saying this?" or, "How might Seneca say this today?" I think many will find revelation in his wisdom.
Such as, in Seneca's, "The Philosopher's Mean" (https://www.lettersfromastoic.net/letter-5-2), he warns one of being so eccentric or different from others, that others lose the ability to empathize and become unperceptive to their ideas. We're more likely to agree with people when we feel understood. And its hard to understand people who actively try to be different than we are. But I can gush about Seneca in another essay (and have).
𝐎𝐧 𝐃𝐢𝐬𝐜𝐮𝐫𝐬𝐢𝐯𝐞𝐧𝐞𝐬𝐬 𝐢𝐧 𝐑𝐞𝐚𝐝𝐢𝐧𝐠
In Seneca's short 3 paragraph essay, "On Discursiveness in Reading" (one of my favorites), Seneca advocates readers be mindful of what they read, and not be frivolous or overly-willing to read any and every book:
> "[...] reading of many authors and books of every sort may tend to make you discursive and unsteady. You must linger among a limited number of master thinkers, and digest their works, if you would derive ideas which shall win firm hold in your mind. Everywhere means nowhere"
When I first read this essay by Seneca, I admit I had something of a severe allergic reaction. One of my greatest neurosis in life is that there are too many good books out there and not enough time to evaluate and read them all.
In fact my life mission (http://mek.fyi/mission) has more or less revolved around the idea of solving this problem -- finding ways to cut through the noise, connect disparate knowledge, reduce redundancy, and ultimately help people think and learn more effectively (much as Vannevar Bush described in his masterfull essay, "As We May Think" https://www.theatlantic.com/.../07/as-we-may-think/303881). I've dedicated a significant portion of my life thinking about books (https://michaelkarpeles.com/.../universal-knowledge/on-books) and I feel completely grateful to live during an age where, when I have a question, I can instantly look it up and usually find answers in one of millions of [web pages, books, and other] resources which I probably didn't even know existed.
And here Seneca is telling us to Marie Kondo our library.
I feel somewhat confident, if this is your first time reading Seneca's essay, that right about now you may be experiencing similar doubts (and I think you should be). Which I think is in part a testament to Seneca's point: that reading things multiple times -- perhaps at multiple stages in life -- can yield meaningful results.
It's worth cutting Seneca a little slack, given his circumstances were vastly different from those we face today. Reading a book during his time was a much greater commitment. Publishing was slow, as was the road for a book to become vetted and popular. There were fewer classics and less reference material or other means for evaluating a book's quality or integrity (harder to detect snake oil). Books were fewer in availability and harder to acquire. Meaning higher risk: choosing one book may be at the expense of another. Fewer recommendations were available (certainly not at societal scale) as were heuristics in general for evaluation. All these things made it easy for a reader to be cynical.
But even still, granting Seneca these indiscretions, his recommendation still feels like it fundamentally runs counter to many of my experiences. Especially being the Silicon Valley Cynic that I am, conditioned to shoot down an idea after finding the first counter example. For instance, I believe a researcher (having been a graduate research myself) is wise to sample the works, ideas, and approaches of a multitude of authors and types in order to obtain a good general understanding of a problem or academic space. Some types of problems require reading a lot of different material, perspectives, and training points. Imagine how difficult it would be, for instance, to learn a new language by re-reading the same book over and over.
And I'm very sure Seneca was aware of these counter-cases. I think it would be myopic and disingenuous to assume Seneca would literally recommend people keep only 10 books on their bookshelf and read them over-and-over again, to the exclusion of others. Rather, I suspect Seneca knew Lucilium (the friend to whom Seneca was writing) was reading his words with the good faith of a caring friend and contemplating his view with an open and interpretive mind. And so to receive its full value, I feel so inclined to pay him a similar courtesy.
𝐇𝐚𝐯𝐢𝐧𝐠 𝐚𝐧 𝐎𝐩𝐞𝐧 𝐌𝐢𝐧𝐝
I like Naval Ravikant's quote: "Don't partner with cynics and pessimists. Their beliefs are self-fulfilling." https://twitter.com/naval/status/1002103360646823936. But I also believe in the immortal words of Open Library's co-founder Aaronsw says:
> "I feel very strongly, that it is not enough to just live in the world as it is and just take what you are given, to follow the things that adults told you to do … and that society tells you to do. I think you should always be questioning. I take this very scientific attitude that everything you’ve learned is just provisional, that it’s always open to recantation, refutation, or questioning"
I think there's a difference between pessimism and cynicism. I believe it's important to be able to see the world cynically, to question everything. But I think it's critical to be able to suspend one's disbelief. In the words of Antonio Gramsci, "pessimism of the intellect optimism of the will". This means being open to ideas and hearing them out. Giving them a fighting chance, even if they seem unpopular, stupid, or implausible. After all, if everyone else is overly quick to dismiss something, your consideration may become your competitive advantage.
In essence, it can be profitable to try and see things others are missing. And listening to others (who think differently, especially in ways which challenge our own thinking) may be the surest way to incorporate signals we've been previously deft to.
So I permitted myself to read Seneca's words a second time, with greater generosity and instead of trying to (easily) find counter-examples, I started to explore all the ways he may be right.
𝐋𝐞𝐚𝐫𝐧𝐢𝐧𝐠𝐬 𝐟𝐫𝐨𝐦 𝐒𝐞𝐧𝐞𝐜𝐚
The first thing which came to mind is the Explore/Exploit Tradeoff. This concept is covered beautifully in Brian Christian's book Algorithms to Live By. In essence the tradeoff questions whether one should stick with what's working or try something new. Should one risk exploring a new restaurant or exploit the one we know we like? There are mathematical principles for making such a decision, e.g. as you explore, are your results going up or down? I think Seneca would advise us to be accountable to such heuristics. And furthermore I think he would advocate for us to fight for our attention. That many books will try to capitalize on our desire to explore, and that this thought is factored into the warnings of his cautionary essay. How many times do you find yourself browsing online, perhaps on facebook, and clicking an article which you know is going to fill your limited-capacity mind with minutia? Twitter may be the best modern day metaphor. I think Seneca would suggest limiting the number of people you follow and setting strict filters to avoid getting distracted by noise.
Humans are susceptible to becoming trapped in paradoxes like Sunk-Cost Fallacy (similar to Explore/Exploit), described well in the book Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion by Robert Cialdini, where one purchases something (like a movie ticket or a book) which turns out to be bad, but on principle one feels obligated to finish it. This is often euphemized as the expression "throwing good money after bad".
I think Patrick Collison (co-founder of Stripe) is likely to agree with the spirit of Seneca's implicit claims.
Patrick says (https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1001898337631588354.html):
> "I discard a lot of books. I like the insight that there's a set of great books that are really worth reading [...] You should skim, you should skip, [...] you should discard and potentially return"
While it's hard to dispute Seneca is literally suggesting one to "limit" oneself to a literary canon, I don't think he's suggesting this canon be immutable and static. After all, one's canon has to originate from somewhere. It doesn't just manifest and thus it must be amenable to addition under the right conditions. I think Seneca is giving us the agency to say no or to question reading a new trendy book e.g. Sapiens or Blink and to quit if a book is determined to be of insufficient quality.
A second clear benefit of reading fewer works more deeply is the opportunity for retention. I think Seneca understands well the power of compound interest and focus, in a way which is now far better understood than in his time. For insight here, look no further than Michael Nielsen's essay on Spaced Repetition (http://augmentingcognition.com/ltm.html).
I think Seneca is on to something, that if instead of wanting to learn e.g. a new language, if your goal is to faithfully emulate a person, then being exposed to their works and their thinking (even their incorrect assumptions) will help you understand their context and circumstances and position you will to continue their work.
I think Seneca's advice though becomes most potent when we relax our assumptions and divorce his ideas from reading books, all together.
> "Food does no good and is not assimilated into the body if it leaves the stomach as soon as it is eaten; nothing hinders a cure so much as frequent change of medicine; no wound will heal when one salve is tried after another; a plant which is often moved can never grow strong. There is nothing so efficacious that it can be helpful while it is being shifted about. And in reading of many books is distraction."
In broad strokes, Seneca is advising focus. Imposing constraints. Abiding by themes. Having a goal. And preparing oneself for compounding interest.
𝐇𝐨𝐰 𝐭𝐡𝐢𝐬 𝐭𝐢𝐞𝐬 𝐭𝐨 𝐆𝐒𝐨𝐂
How is this related to GSoC? And why where you forced to read this essay? Well, first, this essay references a handful of books which I have found very influential to me. And as a fake librarian and perhaps a steward of Seneca's stoic philosophies, I feel compelled to prosthelytize these books with the intent that they may help you better control your destiny and aid you in evaluating which books are worth reading (and how to avoid traps). It's related to GSoC because the number one issue I see in candidates' applications is discursiveness in problem solving.
Candidates will often look through a community's issues (especially issues labeled as high priority or on the active roadmap) and enumerate them on their application. Often times the issues are disparate from each other. One issue will relate to user experience, another will address a metadata problem, and often it's unclear who the benefactor of these bug fixes are.
When I was running a company called Hackerlist with Andrew Valish, there were a lot of problems we were trying to solve. We had a(n overly) complicated 2-sided marketplace where the goal of the company was to allow non-technical companies to contract with remote top software engineering talent. We quickly realized that each of our customers' needs were so different, that we often didn't have engineers with the right skills in our network and so had to go outside of out network , through the expensive process of vetting new engineers, in order to acquire the skills our client needed. Of course, this meant an increase in the amount of time required to serve a client and a lot of overhead in our process. With the advice of Alex Selig and others, we focused our model to serve later stage clients who had a very specific need for researchers with engineering backgrounds who have deep expertise in AI, computer vision, and biometrics. With this change in place, our network of software scientists became elastic and when one wasn't available (or left), another could step in. Or when a company needed to scale to multiple engineers, we were ready to connect them. As Seneca says, if you're everywhere, you're nowhere.
GSoC is no exception to the positive effects of compounding interest. If you're proposed tasks/issues are too disparate in nature, you're missing an opportunity to leverage your previous works to gain momentum. Consider building a house. When you build a strong foundation, this puts you in a better situation to build walls. But if one of your goals is walking the dog and one of your goals is watering the garden and pouring concrete for the driveway, you'll find yourself doing a lot of context switching and coming away with very little compound interest to fuel your future progress. Its easier to push a moving cart than a parked one. Consider focusing on issues which have the capacity of supporting and complimenting each other so you can keep the cart rolling.
This is harder than it seems. Because where do you start? One of my mentors Brad Rubenstein (co-author of "Risk Upfront") gave me the great advice of starting with a theme which represents the outcome you hope to accomplish, not with an issue. What impact you'd like to have? What general, broad problem you'd like to solve? For Open Library in 2019, our general theme has been "Reducing Bad Experiences for Patrons". This theme is still way too broad (it could probably be more specific e.g. "Artificial Intelligence experts who know computer vision and landmark detection") but it at least gives us a foundation for evaluating whether an issue serves our goal. Having a theme may help ensure your issues are related, and there's a greater chance each issue you close moves you in the direction of that goal/outcome
Brewster Kahle, our founder and digital librarian @ Internet Archive, has another piece of sage advice. He suggests "starting with the blog post" and working your way backward.
So by now, your application shouldn't just be a list of high priority issues. It should be a list of important issues which are all coherent and congruent to each other through a theme. But this theme doesn't achieve much if the person reviewing your application isn't aware of it.
1. Your application shouldn't just be a collection of issues. Group issues into logical themes. For instance, "my theme for phase one of my roadmap is making Open Library more accessible to people in non-US countries". Explicitly state what your theme is to your reviewer and why the theme is important to the project. Justify why you believe the theme is the right theme.
2. Ask lots of questions -- don't be too confident, cynical, pessimistic, or close-minded. Your mentor can help you save valuable time by sharing knowledge and perspective. You may feel, for instance, that one of your ideas is easy to implement in principle, but because e.g. Open Library's code base can be difficult to work with, it in reality may take much longer than you think! Ask mentors how long they predict an issue will take. Get feedback on whether the issues you're choosing are the right ones and why, from their perspective.
3. Don't just say yes to anything and everything. Some GSoC applicants are very eager to say "yes" to every recommendation a mentor gives -- mentors want to see candidates who will respectfully fight for the best outcome. As a candidate, you will be bringing a lot of valuable and fresh insight, so if something seems like it is going to be completely unvaluable (the wrong thing to work on), boring, or the solution doesn't seem like the right one, please speak up because you being happy and working on important things is going to be best for everyone. Life is too short to not be learning and working on things we feel are truly important.
4. Try to demonstrate how each issue or milestone you're proposing builds towards and is essential to your desired outcome and is in the service of your theme.
5. Describe what your projected results are when these milestones are complete and what impact it will have towards serving this theme.
A successful application should convince readers what change needs to occur, why the change is important, what impact the changes will have, and how you know if the changes made this impact.
Here's an example of a contrived blurb which is somewhat effective at achieving these points:
> "Open Library is a great website, but basically no one knows about it! I am confident I can change this because I had a similar problem with my website senaca-rocks.org and we were able to improve its ranking by takes cues from the similar but more popular website aristotle-is-dope.org. In open library's case, goodreads is a top 200 website. But Open Library is a top 10,000 website. This must mean people value what goodreads is doing. If you look at how goodreads arranges their website, you'll notice the social `Updates from Friends` feed is prominently shown once you're logged in. Readers must like this feature as a way to discover new books. And this could be an opportunity for Open Library to learn from Goodreads to increase its offering to its patrons. Each of my GSoC milestones below aim to *help more people learn about Open Library* by implementing different parts of a `Follow Friends` pipeline. By the end of the program, I'd like to see 500 unique users following each other and my hypothesis is our alexa rank will go up because of this increased engagement."
I'll probably expand this into a full essay, but these are my raw "good enough" thoughts for now. Hope it helps and provokes further thought. And may some of the books mentioned here enter your literary canon!
Stay focused and good luck!
